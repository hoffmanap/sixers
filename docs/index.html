1. Rim, mid-range, three — and what they're actually worth

Sprawlball's core argument isn't about shooting percentage, it's about expected points per shot. A shot can have a solid FG% and still be a worse decision than a lower-percentage shot from further out.

real FG% + points/shot, our 5 core players combined, 2025-26

Points per shot = FG% × point value (2 for rim/mid, 3 for three). Despite a higher raw FG% than three-pointers, mid-range shots produce fewer expected points than both rim shots and threes — the entire premise of why zone overlap in the mid-range matters more than it looks like on a bar chart.

Rim — 56.8% FG, 1.14 pts/shot. The most efficient shot in the sport by expected value, and the one Maxey and LeBron both live at (48% and 56% of their attempts).
Mid-range — 43.5% FG, 0.87 pts/shot. The lowest expected-value shot on the floor despite a respectable FG% — Embiid and Brown's most-used non-rim zone.

2. Who actually has the ball

What this means for roster construction:
  • Maxey is unambiguously the engine — 28-35% every season, more than double anyone else, every year measured. Whatever else changes, the offense should run through him.
  • Embiid's PRBH share is ~0% every season — he was never a pick-and-roll initiator, and won't become one. His playmaking has to come from the post/short-roll, a different action entirely, which means the offense needs two initiation systems, not one.
  • LeBron's ball-handling has genuinely declined (20.8% → 11.7% since 2022-23) — a real, data-backed signal that his role should keep shrinking toward off-ball and secondary creation, not stay flat.

What this means: Maxey (4.26 mph avg.) is meaningfully faster than Embiid (3.67 mph) — the widest speed gap on the roster between its two most important two-man pairing. But the net-rating data tells a different story than "pace mismatch": their on-court numbers collapsed only when Embiid was hurt (2024-25, -3.2), not because of a speed gap that exists in every season. Edgecombe (4.25 mph) profiles as the closer speed-match if the team wants a Maxey-caliber pace option that doesn't force Embiid to keep up.
The three bench questions you actually care about — answered with real data:
  • Does Dean Wade actually achieve the spacing he was signed for? Yes, clearly. 70.5% of Wade's shots last season were threes — the single most Sprawlball-compliant shot profile of anyone in this entire study, starters included — at a real 36.2% clip. And his defended-FG%-differential (-2.3) backs up the "stopper" half of the signing too. The one surprise: his real average speed (4.53 mph) was actually faster than Maxey's — he's not just a stand-and-shoot floor spacer, he can keep pace in transition.
  • Do Simons and KCP address specific issues? Partially, and differently. Simons (58.5% of shots from three, 38.5% 3P%) is a legitimate movement/pull-up shooter who adds guard-position shot creation the bench previously lacked — but his defended-FG%-differential (+1.2) is a mild minus, so he's an offense-only addition. KCP is the bigger question mark: his shot profile (47.5% three, only 31.6% 3P%) is below what his 3&D reputation would suggest, and his defended-FG%-differential (+8.6) is the single worst number of any player in this study — a real red flag worth tracking early in the season rather than assuming the reputation still holds.
  • Who actually backs up Embiid, with Drummond gone? Bona and Hukporti profile almost identically on shot selection (96.8% and 93.8% of shots at the rim, respectively — pure roller/dunker types), but the evidence differs sharply. Bona already has real Sixers on-court data: +3.2 net rating in 766 minutes next to Maxey this season, up from -2.7 the year before — a real, improving track record. Hukporti has zero shared minutes with this core yet (he's a new addition) but posted the best individual defensive number of anyone in the entire study (-9.4) on a small 80-shot sample — promising, but unproven in this specific system. Bona is the safer bet; Hukporti is the higher-variance one.

Note: Wade, Simons, and KCP's numbers above reflect their last season with their previous team (they signed with Philadelphia in the 2026 offseason) — real evidence of what they bring, not yet Sixers on-court data. Bona's lineup numbers are real Sixers data since he's been on the roster since 2024-25.

5. So — will it work?

Pulling every finding above together: this roster's floor is high because of individual talent, but its ceiling depends on solving problems that are visible in the data right now, not hypothetical.

Reasons for optimism

  • Maxey + Embiid is real, and it's good. Best net rating of any 600+-minute duo on the 2025-26 team (+7.2), and the collapse in 2024-25 tracks Embiid's health, not a scheme incompatibility.
  • Maxey and Edgecombe give this team two legitimately Sprawlball-compliant guards — under 15% mid-range each, plus real defensive tools, plus similar top-end speed (4.26 vs. 4.25 mph).
  • Embiid's post-up efficiency (79th percentile) and Brown's isolation efficiency (77th percentile) mean the "bad" shot types aren't actually bad for them — the problem is purely spatial, which is more coachable than a talent or efficiency problem.

Reasons for concern

  • Brown's mid-range rate nearly doubled this season (14.3% → 23.7%) at the exact moment he's joining a roster that needs him spacing the floor — the single biggest shot-profile swing in the dataset, moving the wrong way.
  • Four players with a real claim to touches, one ball. Maxey, LeBron, Brown, and Embiid all show meaningful usage/initiation numbers — nobody's arc points toward a clean hierarchy yet.
  • LeBron is a measurable minus in three different data cuts now — declining PnR frequency, declining post-up efficiency, and a positive (bad) defended-FG% differential. At 40, that's an aging curve showing up in the numbers, not a hunch.

What the Sixers actually have to figure out

  • Get Brown's shot profile back toward 2022-24 levels. His own efficiency doesn't demand it, but the offense's floor spacing does — every mid-range Brown iso is a possession where Maxey's driving lanes and Embiid's short-roll space both get worse.
  • Stagger Embiid and Brown's touches, not just their minutes. Both operate heavily in the same restricted-area/mid-range band (see the overlap chart above) — the fix isn't necessarily less Embiid or less Brown, it's not running both of their preferred actions in the same possession.
  • Decide LeBron's role explicitly, and shrink it on schedule. The numbers already show him trending toward a play-finisher/secondary-creator role — formalizing that now, rather than mid-season, protects both the offense's spacing and his own defensive minutes.
  • Bet on health more than scheme. The single largest swing in any dataset here — Maxey+Embiid's net rating — tracks Embiid's games played almost exactly. If he plays 65+ games, most of the spacing questions above become manageable; if he plays 30, no amount of shot-profile tinkering fixes it.

Prediction: this roster has enough two-way talent to be a top-6 offense and a middling-to-good defense if health cooperates — but its ceiling (championship contender vs. good-but-flawed) hinges specifically on whether Brown's shot profile reverts toward his Boston norm and whether Embiid plays 60+ games. Those two variables matter more to this team's outcome than anything about the LeBron signing.
